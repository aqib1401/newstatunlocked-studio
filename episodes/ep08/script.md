SCRIPT:
What if you could never forget anything? Not a name, not a single second. Day one, your teacher asks what she wore on your first day of school, three years ago. You tell her, and the color of her socks. Day four, at the mall, a little girl is lost. You saw her mom three hours ago, green scarf, by the fountain, so you walk her there, and her mom bursts into tears. But that night, you can't sleep. Every bad moment replays in full color. Waving at someone who was waving at the person behind you. The last day with your dog. On repeat. Because here's what nobody tells you. Forgetting isn't a flaw. While you sleep, your brain trims most of the day's details, so the important stuff stands out. A few real people remember almost every day of their lives, and some say it's like a movie that never stops. By day thirty, you barely sleep at all. Your doctor can switch it off tonight. Back to normal. Blurry. Your grandma is two floors up. She's forgotten almost everyone, even you. But when you tell her old stories back, word for word, she lights up. Grandma whispers, Tell me the one about the lake again. Turn it off, and her stories blur like everything else. The nurse calls your name. Ten seconds. Nine. Eight. Grandma squeezes your hand. Seven. Six. Your dog's last day, still replaying. Five. Four. Her stories, or your sleep. Which do you choose?

FACTS USED:
1. Hyperthymesia (highly superior autobiographical memory) is real: a small number of people recall almost every day of their lives: high
2. Some people with it describe their memories as constantly running, like a movie they can't stop: medium-high
3. Forgetting is useful: during sleep the brain weakens/prunes many of the day's connections so important memories stand out: medium-high
4. People with memory loss in old age often still respond to familiar old stories (older memories tend to last longest): medium-high
